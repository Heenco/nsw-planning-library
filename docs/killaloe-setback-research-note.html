Six Metres at Killaloe Avenue

Why the library shows four front setbacks for one Hornsby lot, what the 2024–26 research says about pulling conditional, spatial rules out of DCP documents, and how to close the gap starting with this address.

Prompted by the 6 Killalore Ave email (cadastre spelling: Killaloe Avenue) Source instrument Hornsby DCP 2024, Table 3.1.2-a Data checked planningai: up_property_d_4, rule_effect, lot_frontage_run

The short answer

The email is right, and the failure is one we have already diagnosed in docs/dcp-extraction-review.md: the extractor reads every number in the cell and stores five front setbacks under a single rule with a single scope. Nothing in the schema can say which number belongs to which road. It is not a parsing failure. Docling captured the cell verbatim, bullets and all. It is not a data failure either. Annexure C of the same DCP lists the designated roads, and the cadastre already names the frontage road, its hierarchy and the suburb for every Hornsby lot. It is a shape failure: a control cell is a small decision table, and we store it as a bag of values.

For the address in question, every input the rule needs is in the database today. The verdict panel below is what the panel should say, and each row can be evaluated from columns we already hold. The one clause we cannot evaluate is the streetscape condition (conform to an existing 7.6 m building line where one exists), which needs the neighbours' building footprints. The honest output for that row is “check on site”, not silence and not a fifth number.

The research supports the direction the review already set, and sharpens three points. Parsing is solved well enough for our documents; the bottleneck is the grain at which rules are stored. The extraction step must be constrained to a closed vocabulary of predicates that something in our stack can resolve, must carry a verbatim sub-span per case, and must run through a judge-and-repair loop; even then, plan for 15–20% of cells needing human review. And the language model must never do the geography: it names the predicate at extraction time, and PostGIS evaluates it at query time against materialised columns. No vendor and no NSW standard exists to adopt. The closest published precedents are ACCORD's RASE tooling, the De Jure regulatory-rule pipeline, PlanX's outcome flags, and Zoneomics' December 2025 replacement of “see text” with conditional control values.

What the 2024–26 research says

Four sub-problems hide inside “extract the setback from the DCP”: read the table faithfully, turn the cell into rules rather than numbers, ground the rules' conditions in geography, and evaluate them per parcel with exceptions and unknowns. The literature is uneven across the four. The first is close to solved, the second is active and measurable, the third is where published work is weakest and our own assets are strongest, and the fourth is settled practice in a handful of products and standards worth copying.

1. Reading tables: solved well enough, and not our bottleneck

SourceWhat it showsNumbers
Benchmarking PDF parsers on table extraction with LLM-based semantic evaluation (arXiv 2603.18652, 2026)21 parsers on real tables. Vision-language models lead; rule-based tools fail; several parsers are bimodal, either perfect or omitting the table. Failure modes: flattened multi-level headers, resolved rowspans, lost decimals. An LLM judge tracks human judgment far better than TEDS.Gemini 3 Pro 9.55, Gemini 3 Flash 9.50, LightOnOCR-2-1B 9.08, Mistral OCR 3 8.89, GPT-5 mini 7.14, Claude Sonnet 4.6 7.02, MinerU2.5 6.49 (0–10). Judge r = 0.93 vs TEDS 0.68.
PulseBench-Tab (arXiv 2606.07534, 2026)Graph-based table evaluation that includes cells holding lists, bullets and multi-line content, which is exactly the shape of the Hornsby cell. Parsers differ most on intra-cell structure.—
OmniDocBench (CVPR 2025)Attribute-level table tags: merged cells, rotation, formulas in cells. Rotated tables are the worst failure mode measured.—
Docling TableFormer; MinerU2.5 (2025)Docling's structure model is strong on clean grids and gives page provenance; MinerU emits HTML that keeps rowspan and colspan where Docling's markdown flattens them.Docling TEDS above 91% on FinTabNet (vendor and blog comparisons, not peer-reviewed).
Verdict. Keep Docling for structure and provenance. Add a second read of control tables only by a vision-language model in the Gemini 3 Flash class, diff the two, and store cells as structured cells with span columns, never as markdown. This is section 5.7 of the review; the benchmark evidence says it is cheap and the risk it removes is real.

2. Rules, not numbers: schema-constrained extraction with spans, judged and repaired

SourceWhat it showsNumbers
RASE (Hjelseth & Nisbet, CIB W78 2011) and ACCORD D2.4 Semantics Documentation (Horizon Europe, Aug 2025)Requirement / Applicability / Selection / Exception marked on the text, each with (object, property, comparator, value, unit). ACCORD ships the AEC3PO ontology, a Rule Formalisation Tool, a Building Compliance Rule Language and a RASE-LLM annotator emitting YAML-LD. The table rule is explicit: applicability from the upmost and leftmost cells, each interior cell a distinct test.No public accuracy figures for RASE-LLM.
CODE-ACCORD corpus (Scientific Data, 2024)Annotated building regulations (England, Finland) with 4 entity and 10 relation types. Even expert annotators disagree a lot, which bounds what any extractor can be scored against.862 sentences, 4,297 entities, 4,329 relations; entity inter-annotator agreement 0.37.
De Jure: iterative LLM self-refinement for structured extraction of regulatory rules (arXiv 2604.02276, 2026)Docling, then LLM rule units (action, conditions, constraints, exceptions, penalties, verbatim span), then three LLM judges over 19 criteria, then selective regeneration of failing stages. Cross-domain without retuning. No human gold standard, which the authors admit.4.71–4.85 / 5 across SEC, HIPAA, EU AI Act; non-hallucination 5.00; downstream QA win rate 74–84%.
From legal text to executable decision models (arXiv 2604.17153, 2026)GPT-5.1 generating DMN decision models from Dutch environmental regulation. Telling the model the expected inputs and outputs was the largest lever by far; semantic enrichment of the text barely helped. Structural similarity did not guarantee the same outcomes.95 gold models; +37–54% structural similarity with I/O spec; outcome equivalence 51–53%; best runs 33% perfectly equivalent.
Legal text to Defeasible Deontic Logic via LLMs (arXiv 2506.08899, 2025); Catala; s(CASP)Exceptions modelled as defeasible defaults (“6 m unless designated road”) rather than nested IFs, with explanation of which rule won. Failure modes: nested conditions, implicit obligations, scope of pronouns.—
P4IR: SFT + GRPO for automated code compliance (arXiv 2606.22402, 2026)A fine-tuned small model beats frontier models used few-shot at producing rule code skeletons from building regulations; reinforcement learning cut false positives.Up to −23.8% tree-edit distance and −38.6% Levenshtein vs SFT; beats Claude Opus/Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.2 few-shot.
Systematic review of methods for interpreting building code regulations (Building Research & Information, 2026)Rule-based and ontology approaches are deterministic and traceable but do not scale; LLMs scale but hallucinate; the frontier is hybrids with deterministic verification.—
Verdict. The DMN paper's “give the model the I/O spec” is our guard registry: constrain the extractor to a closed list of predicates it may use, and to the value/unit/comparator fields already in rule_effect. Require a verbatim sub-span per case, not per cell. Run De Jure-style judge and repair, then deterministic verification (value literal in span, span literal in cell, guard in registry, cases exhaustive and mutually exclusive), and route what fails to a review queue. The published numbers say 80–85% clean is realistic; 98% is not.

3. Grounding text to geography: the model names the predicate, the database evaluates it

SourceWhat it showsNumbers
GS-QA geospatial question answering benchmark (arXiv 2605.22811, 2026)Text-to-SQL over PostGIS is the best of three approaches and still fails most questions; dense retrieval is worst because embeddings ignore location; directional and multi-source predicates are near random.Claude Sonnet 4.6 text-to-SQL: 44.2% correct, 89.7% valid SQL; GPT-4o 33.0%.
GeoSQL-Eval / GeoSQL-Bench (Expert Systems with Applications, 2026)First systematic evaluation of LLMs writing PostGIS. Frontier models struggle with spatial functions and schema retrieval.14,178 instances, 340 PostGIS functions, 82 databases.
Coordinates from Context (arXiv 2510.08741, 2025)LLMs reason well over coordinates they are given and badly over coordinates they must recall. A recaller (gazetteer or geocoder) plus an LLM reasoner beats either alone.36 km error reasoning over supplied coordinates vs 200+ km generating them.
Spatial-RAG (arXiv 2502.18470, 2025); toponym resolution with small LLMs and gazetteers (IJGIS 2024)Hybrid retrieval: a spatial database does the sparse geometric filter, the model does semantic ranking. Gazetteer knowledge is what makes LLM geoparsing work at all.—
Verdict. Never let the model compute geography at query time. At extraction time it emits frontage_road_class = designated from the registry; at query time PostGIS evaluates that against materialised columns. A document-internal gazetteer such as Annexure C is better grounded than any external geoparser, but only once its From/To extents are geometry. Cross-references to maps (“HLEP Foreshore Building Line Map”) are the same mechanism and map_layer already handles them.

4. Exceptions and unknowns: four outcomes, and an explanation with every number

SourceWhat it shows
Open Digital Planning / PlanX and the digital-planning-data-schemas (UK, 2025–26)Rules authored by planning authorities as flowcharts, with four outcomes: missingInfo, failsToMeetPolicy, edgeCase, meetsPolicy. Non-applicable constraints stay in the payload with intersects: false, so absence is asserted rather than inferred.
MHCLG planning data standards, regulations laid April 2026First mandatory data standards under the 2023 Act cover plan timetables and housing requirements only. Policies themselves are still not published as data, even in the most advanced jurisdiction.
New Zealand National Planning Standards (ePlans, 2019 onward)A legislated requirement that a plan be queryable by property; provisions carry stable identifiers (for example RLZ-R1) that survive amendment. Rangitīkei's ePlan went operative January 2025.
Zoneomics, December 2025 releaseThe only national-scale zoning product to ship conditional controls: conditional_control_values=true replaces the “STF” (see text field) sentinel with the logic behind it. A scalar-only schema degenerated into an escape hatch, as ours has.
Symbium (computational law, California ADU rules)Parcel-level evaluation with explanation: the model turns red and says why. The explanation, not the number, is the product.
Verdict. Store and return (value, guard that selected it, clause, objective). Unresolved evaluates to “unknown”, never to “non-compliant”, which is also what s4.15(3A) requires of a DCP control. The panel answers with one row and a reason, and collapses the other branches behind “why this number”.

5. NSW and the vendors: nobody to buy this from

Nothing found in 2026 changes the picture in the review. The NSW Planning Portal still requires councils to publish DCPs as documents, with technical requirements only for the spatial datasets inside them (in force since November 2015). The Pattern Book Development Code and the Housing Code sit inside the Codes SEPP, which is why every vendor's rules engine covers them and no vendor's covers DCP prose. PropCode returns DCPs as PDF blobs; Feasibly labels its NSW DCP tier “screening-grade”; Archistar claims rules “displayed conditionally”. The Geoscape Buildings footprint dataset, which the streetscape clause needs, is now distributed free to NSW government agencies through DCS Spatial Services and commercially to everyone else, so the last unresolvable guard has a known price.

Recommended approach

The order matters: prove the answer with the data we hold before changing the schema, and change the schema before spending on model extraction. Each phase has an acceptance test on real lots, because coverage counts have already fooled us once (99.9% of sections had a rule; clause 3.1.2 had four wrong numbers).

  1. Prove the answer with existing datadays

    Hand-encode the two cells of Table 3.1.2-a as cases. Implement three guards: frontage_road_class (Annexure C names × primary_frontage_road, filtered by suburb as an interim proxy for extents), suburb, and site_ref. Wire them into the frontage-dcp panel with the reason line.

    Acceptance: 6 Killaloe Ave gives 6 m and 7.6 m with the streetscape flag; 30 Casuarina Dr, Cherrybrook gives 9 m; a Pennant Hills Road lot gives 9 m; a George Street, Pennant Hills lot gives 6 m (the collision test).

  2. Put the case grain in the schema1–2 weeks

    A rule_case row per branch (R / A / S / E in RASE terms) carrying guard triples (predicate, operator, value) that reference a guard_registry with its resolver and status (resolved, ask the proposal, unresolvable). Each case keeps its own verbatim sub-span. Add span columns to section_table_cell. Add the four-valued outcome to the resolver.

  3. LLM extraction of cells into cases2–4 weeks

    Context packet per cell: cell text, row and column headers, caption, heading chain, and the DCP's own definition of any term it uses (the “Designated roads” paragraph). Schema-constrained JSON limited to registry predicates. Judge and repair, then deterministic verification, then audit_finding rows for whatever fails. Target the 178 open ambiguous_cell findings across Hornsby and Randwick (10 are road-class, 14 lot width, 12 corner or battle-axe, 46 parking rates that need their own model).

  4. Resolvers that need geometryin parallel

    Annexure C extents to road-segment geometry via linear referencing on the NSW Road Segment layer. Foreshore Building Line map join for the waterfront row. Prevailing building line from Geoscape Buildings footprints; until then the guard stays declared unresolvable and the panel says “check on site”.

  5. Presentation and the accuracy gatewith each phase

    One answer per control with its guard and objective. Extend planningai-datum-check.mjs with the four acceptance addresses so the gate fails loudly on regression. Label the grade of every number: resolved from data, needs proposal input, or needs site check.

A reply to send back

Thanks, this is the exact gap we measured in the extraction review. The extractor keeps every number in that cell (3, 6, 7.6, 9, 9) but has nowhere to store the condition that picks between them, so the panel shows all of them. The good news: every input for that address (Killaloe Avenue in the cadastre) is already in our data. The frontage road and its class, the suburb, and the DCP's own Annexure C list of designated roads. With those, the answer is 6 m for a dwelling and 7.6 m for an attached dual occupancy. The “existing 7.6 m streetscape” clause needs neighbours' building footprints we don't hold yet, so we'd show it as “check on site” rather than guess.

I've written up what the current research says. Short version: parsing isn't the problem, the rule grain is. The fix is a decision-table shape per cell, a closed list of spatial predicates our database can actually resolve, LLM extraction constrained to that list with verbatim spans and a verification pass, and PostGIS doing the geography. I'd like to prove it on Hornsby clause 3.1.2 first, with your address as the acceptance test.
